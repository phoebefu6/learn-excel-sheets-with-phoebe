Learn Excel & Sheets with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Types that lie

Three cells can show the same customer ID and hold three different things. A date column can look like dates on every row and be text on every row. Nothing turns red, because a spreadsheet is happy to compare a number with a string and tell you they differ. Tonight we read the type as well as the value, find out why the same file gives a different late-delivery share on two laptops, and rebuild both columns on purpose instead of trusting the app to guess.

Part 1 · covers numbers stored as text

Numbers that are really text

5 min live

Session 1 showed that a cell holds a value and a type. Here is what that costs. A lookup, a COUNTIFS and a pivot all compare cells, and a comparison asks two questions: are the characters the same, and is the kind of thing the same? The number 7060 and the text "7060 " fail the second question, and the screen will not tell you which one you are looking at.

LiveVALUE, TRIM, and the order they go in3 min▶

Ferncastle's orders.csv has 94 customer IDs stored as text. 67 are zero-padded by the system that exported them: order 10004 belongs to customer 3082 and arrives as "03082". 27 carry a trailing space: order 10009 belongs to customer 7060 and arrives as "7060 ". An exact lookup against customers.csv, where both IDs are numbers, returns #N/A for all 94, and IFERROR from session 2 turns each one into a blank.

Microsoft's help page on numbers stored as text converts them with VALUE. Its TRIM page says TRIM "Removes all spaces from text except for single spaces between words", and Google's says TRIM "Removes leading, trailing, and repeated spaces in text". Put them together and the order matters: =VALUE(TRIM(B2)) first strips the space, then turns the digits into a number. "03082" becomes 3082, "7060 " becomes 7060, and a cell that was already a number comes out unchanged. That formula works in both apps.

Self-studyThe space TRIM does not see3 min▶

There is more than one space character. The one your space bar types is character 32. Web pages and some emails use the nonbreaking space, character 160, to stop a line wrapping between two words, and it comes along when you copy and paste. Microsoft's TRIM page says plainly that TRIM does not remove the nonbreaking space (decimal 160) on its own. Google's TRIM page describes spaces without mentioning character 160 at all, so in Sheets the honest position is: test it on your own cell before you rely on it.

The common fix is to turn character 160 into an ordinary space first, then trim: =VALUE(TRIM(SUBSTITUTE(B2, CHAR(160), " "))). Ferncastle's 27 trailing spaces are all ordinary ones, which is why plain VALUE(TRIM()) takes its missing rows to 0. Keep the longer form for data that has passed through a browser.

Self-studyCheck the type before you trust the column2 min▶

Two counts settle it. =SUMPRODUCT(--ISTEXT(B2:B1566)) counts the text cells in the key column, =SUMPRODUCT(--ISNUMBER(B2:B1566)) counts the numbers. On Ferncastle's orders.csv the first is 94 and the second is the rest of the 1,565 rows. If both counts are above zero, the column is two columns sharing a header, and every lookup that touches it will quietly split its rows into "found" and "not found" by type rather than by customer.

Run the same check on the other side of the join. A lookup table can hold a text key too, and then the numbers in your orders are the ones that miss. Session 6 has a button that does exactly that to one customer, and nothing on the screen changes except the totals.

Real world

The export that padded its own IDs. Ferncastle's 67 zero-padded IDs are the most ordinary trap in data work: a system writes IDs at a fixed width so they sort as text, and the spreadsheet on the other end reads most of the file as numbers. Each cell is perfectly reasonable on its own; the damage only appears when the two files meet in a lookup, where 94 rows (67 padded, 27 with a trailing space) find nobody and the revenue they carry drops out of every state.

Part 2 · covers dates stored as text

Dates that depend on who opens the file

5 min live

A spreadsheet date is a number counting days, dressed up by a format. A date written as text is just characters, and turning characters into a date means deciding which part is the day and which is the month. DATEVALUE does not decide from the file. It decides from a setting on the computer.

LiveOne file, two laptops, two answers3 min▶

Ferncastle's delivered_date is day-first text on every one of its 1,565 rows: order 10002 was delivered on "11/07/2017", the eleventh of July. Microsoft's DATEVALUE page warns that results can vary with "your computer's system date setting"; Google's says "Understood formats may depend on region and language settings". On a month-first setting, the setting common in the US, "11/07/2017" becomes 7 November 2017, a real date four months late, and "13/06/2017" becomes #VALUE!, because there is no thirteenth month.

So the same formula on the same file prints different late shares depending on whose laptop opens it. Dev's IFERROR treats every #VALUE! as "not late", and every swapped date in the first twelve days of a month is compared with the estimate as if it were real. The bench models this with a month-first setting and labels it modelled; on a day-first setting your naive numbers will differ, and may even come out right, which is the most dangerous outcome of all: a formula that only works for you.

Self-studyHow to tell a date from text that looks like one3 min▶

The same two counts from Part 1 work on dates, because a real date is a number: =SUMPRODUCT(--ISNUMBER(E2:E1566)) should equal the row count for a date column you can trust. On Ferncastle's delivered_date, imported as text, it is 0. The estimated_date column next to it is ISO, YYYY-MM-DD, which leaves no doubt about which part is the month; check whether your app turned it into a number on import with =ISNUMBER(D2).

Two more habits are common practice. Never judge a date column by how it is aligned or formatted, since a format can be applied to text and a number can be shown as anything. And when a file arrives from another country, ask what order its dates are written in before you open it, then import that column as text so nothing guesses on your behalf.

Real world

Genes renamed so a spreadsheet would leave them alone. Some human gene symbols look like dates: SEPT1, MARCH1. Typed or pasted into Excel, they were auto-converted to dates. In 2020 the HGNC guidelines (Bruford et al., Nature Genetics) recorded that "all symbols that auto-converted to dates in Microsoft Excel have been changed": "SEPT1 is now SEPTIN1; MARCH1 is now MARCHF1". How widespread the damage had been is reported, not read here: Ziemann, Eren and El-Osta (2016) reported that about one fifth of papers with supplementary Excel gene lists contained gene-name conversion errors. The scientists did not win the argument with the software; they renamed the genes.

Part 3 · covers rebuilding types on purpose

Rebuild the type, don't guess it

5 min live

The fix for both columns is the same idea: stop asking the app to infer a type, and build the type you want from parts you can see. For the key, strip and convert. For the date, cut the text into day, month and year by position and hand them to DATE in the order DATE expects.

LiveDATE(RIGHT, MID, LEFT)3 min▶

Day-first text has a fixed shape: two digits of day, a slash, two digits of month, a slash, four digits of year. Position does the parsing. LEFT(E2,2) is the day, MID(E2,4,2) the month, RIGHT(E2,4) the year. DATE takes them as year, month, day:

=DATE(RIGHT(E2,4), MID(E2,4,2), LEFT(E2,2))

"08/06/2017" becomes 8 June 2017 on every computer, whatever its setting, in both apps. Format the column as a date so you can read it; underneath it is now a number, and ISNUMBER says TRUE. The formula relies on the shape, so check it holds: =SUMPRODUCT(--(LEN(E2:E1566)<>10)) should be 0 before you trust it. On Ferncastle's file it is.

Self-studyWhat the late share was hiding3 min▶

Dev's late flag uses one rule, chosen here and written down: an order is late when its delivered date is after its estimated date. On the naive and exact builds, with DATEVALUE under a month-first setting, 332 rows carry the wrong flag and the late share reads 17.76%. Rebuild the date and the wrong flags go to 0: the share over all 1,565 rows is 7.03%. Keep one row per order with session 1's running count and it is 7.23%, which is 109 late orders out of 1,508.

Notice what did not happen. No order arrived earlier. No formula errored on screen, because IFERROR had swallowed every #VALUE!. The 17.76% looked like a respectable operations number and would have gone to Imogen on Monday. The only defence is the one from session 1: say what the column holds, check its type, and reconcile the result against a second route.

Real world

Imogen's late-delivery share. On Ferncastle's own rows the naive build reports 17.76% of deliveries late and the cleaned build 7.23%. A number two and a half times too high is the kind that starts a supplier review or a courier change. Both numbers came out of the same file and the same rule; only the types differed.

Write the cleaned column next to the raw one, never over it. Keep customer_id and delivered_date exactly as they arrived, and add clean_customer_id and delivered as new columns. A reviewer can then put the raw and the rebuilt value side by side on any row and see what your formula did.

Build-along

Clean the key and the date in Ferncastle's orders

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open the same three CSVs you downloaded in session 1, in Excel or Google Sheets, with customers.csv on its own sheet named customers. orders.csv runs A order_id, B customer_id, C purchase_date, D estimated_date, E delivered_date, F payment_seq, G payment_type, H payment_value, I order_value, and J is the running count you added in session 1. Import the file rather than double-clicking it, and keep customer_id and delivered_date as text if your import offers the choice: if step 1 does not print 94, your app converted cells on the way in, and that is the lesson of tonight arriving early.

Count the types in the key column. Before fixing anything, measure the problem. Both formulas work in both apps.

★ Step 1 · how many keys are textText keys: =SUMPRODUCT(--ISTEXT(B2:B1566)) -> 94 Numeric keys: =SUMPRODUCT(--ISNUMBER(B2:B1566)) the two add up to 1565 94 = 67 zero-padded ("03082") + 27 trailing space ("7060 ")

Make the missing rows visible. Repeat session 2's exact lookup on the raw key, with a label instead of a blank so the misses can be counted.

★ Step 2 · the raw lookup (column K)K1: state_raw K2: =IFERROR(VLOOKUP(B2, customers!$A:$C, 3, FALSE), "NOT FOUND") fill down to K1566 Count: =COUNTIFS(K2:K1566, "NOT FOUND") -> 94

Clean the key and look up again. Add the cleaned key next to the raw one, never over it, and point the lookup at the clean column.

★ Step 3 · clean key (L) and state (M)L1: clean_customer_id L2: =VALUE(TRIM(B2)) M1: state M2: =IFERROR(VLOOKUP(L2, customers!$A:$C, 3, FALSE), "NOT FOUND") fill both down to row 1566 Count: =COUNTIFS(M2:M1566, "NOT FOUND") -> 0 Row 6 (order 10004): B "03082" -> L 3082 -> M MS

See what the naive date does on your machine. This is the formula most people write. Check column D first: if =ISNUMBER(D2) is TRUE your app already made the ISO date a number, so drop the DATEVALUE around D2. The bench's answer is modelled under a month-first setting; on a day-first setting your numbers will differ.

★ Step 4 · naive late flag (column P)P1: late_naive P2: =IFERROR(DATEVALUE(E2) > DATEVALUE(D2), FALSE) fill down to P1566 Share of rows: =SUMPRODUCT(--P2:P1566) / 1565 -> 17.76% under a month-first setting (modelled)

Rebuild the date from its parts. Same comparison, but the delivered date is now built by position, so no setting is involved. Format column N as a date to read it.

★ Step 5 · rebuilt date (N) and late flag (O)N1: delivered N2: =DATE(RIGHT(E2,4), MID(E2,4,2), LEFT(E2,2)) O1: late O2: =N2 > D2 (or N2 > DATEVALUE(D2) if D is text) fill both down to row 1566 Shape check: =SUMPRODUCT(--(LEN(E2:E1566)<>10)) -> 0 Share of rows: =SUMPRODUCT(--O2:O1566) / 1565 -> 7.03% Flags changed: =SUMPRODUCT(--(O2:O1566<>P2:P1566)) -> 332 (month-first)

Count orders, not payments. 7.03% is still a share of rows, and orders.csv has one row per payment. Use the running count from session 1 to keep the first row of each order.

★ Step 6 · late share per orderLate orders: =SUMPRODUCT((J2:J1566=1) * O2:O1566) -> 109 Orders: =COUNTIFS(J2:J1566, 1) -> 1508 Late share: 109 / 1508 -> 7.23% Missing rows 94 -> 0 · late share 17.76% -> 7.23%

Leave column P in place for now. The naive flag next to the rebuilt one is your evidence: filter for rows where they differ and you are looking at the 332 rows that would have gone into Monday's number with the wrong answer.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A key cell shows 7060 and holds the text "7060 " with a trailing ordinary space. Which formula turns it into a number an exact lookup can match?

TRIM returns text, so "7060" without the space is still text and still misses the number 7060. VALUE after TRIM produces the number. In Ferncastle's orders.csv this takes the NOT FOUND rows from 94 to 0. If the space were a nonbreaking one, character 160, Microsoft says TRIM would not remove it.

2 · delivered_date holds the text "11/07/2017", meaning 11 July. On a computer with a month-first date setting, what does DATEVALUE do with it, and with "13/06/2017"?

Microsoft and Google both say DATEVALUE depends on the computer's date or region settings. Under a month-first setting, days 1 to 12 swap silently and days 13 to 31 error. With IFERROR around it, neither shows on screen.

3 · Dev's naive late share is 17.76%. After rebuilding the date with DATE(RIGHT, MID, LEFT) and keeping one row per order, what is it, and why did it move?

The rebuilt date fixes the flags, giving 7.03% over all 1,565 rows. Those rows are payments, not orders; keeping the first row of each order with session 1's running count gives 7.23%, which matches the truth on the bench.
