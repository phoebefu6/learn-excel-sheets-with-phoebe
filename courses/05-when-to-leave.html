Part 1 · covers limits read at source

The ceilings, as the vendors state them

5 min live

Every analyst has heard a number for "how big a spreadsheet can get", and most of those numbers are out of date. Read them at source. The two apps do not even measure the same thing: Excel caps each worksheet by rows and columns, Google Sheets caps the whole spreadsheet by cells or file size. That difference changes which files stop fitting.

LiveTwo numbers, read where they are published3 min▶

Microsoft's "Excel specifications and limits" page gives the worksheet size as 1,048,576 rows by 16,384 columns. That is per worksheet: a file with three tabs has three of those grids. Google Drive Help gives the Sheets limit as "Up to 20 million cells or 100MB" for spreadsheets created in or converted to Sheets. That is per spreadsheet, counted across its cells, so a wide file runs out of room in fewer rows than a narrow one. If you remember a figure of 10 million cells for Sheets, it is out of date; quote the 20 million figure, and quote the page.

Ferncastle's orders.csv is 1,565 rows across columns A to I. That is well under one percent of either ceiling. For a build like this one, the ceiling is not the problem; the things in the next part are. Knowing the ceiling still matters, for one reason: the day a file crosses it, nothing on the screen looks different.

Self-studyWhat happens at the edge, and what we do not know3 min▶

The vendor pages state the ceilings. They are the source for how big a sheet can be, not for what each app does when you open a file that is bigger, and that behaviour is not recorded here, so do not teach it from memory either. What you can do without knowing it is check. Count the rows in the source before you import (the export's own row count, or a line count in a shell) and count them again in the sheet with =COUNTA(A:A). If the two counts differ, the sheet is not holding the file, whatever the reason.

That is the same reconciliation habit from session 1, pointed at the import instead of the pivot. A control total does not care whether rows went missing because of a lookup, a filter or a ceiling. It only tells you that they did.

Real world

A size limit nobody saw. Public Health England said in October 2020 that 15,841 positive COVID-19 cases between 25 September and 2 October "were not included" in reported figures, and gave the cause as "some files containing positive test results exceeded the maximum file size". Read the statement and notice what it does not say: it does not name Excel and it does not mention a row limit. Press reports did. The lesson that survives either reading is the one you can act on: a size ceiling dropped records, and the published number looked complete until it was reconciled.

Part 2 · covers better reasons to leave than size

Repeat, review, joins, then size

5 min live

A spreadsheet is a superb place to find an answer once. It is a weaker place to produce the same answer every week, to let someone else check how you got it, or to hold a chain of lookups that is really a join nobody wrote down. Those three reasons arrive long before the row limit does. The four questions below are a working rule assembled here from the earlier sessions, not a vendor recommendation.

LiveRepetition and review are the real triggers3 min▶

Count what Dev does each Monday. Import two files. Clean the customer IDs with VALUE(TRIM()). Rebuild the delivery dates with DATE(RIGHT, MID, LEFT). Look up the state with an exact lookup. Fill the running count. Refresh the pivot with the first-row filter. Check the control gap. Seven manual steps, most of which guarded against a trap found in sessions 1 to 4, and each of which can be skipped by a tired person at 08:50 on a Monday without the sheet noticing. A script or a stored query runs the same seven steps the same way every time.

Review is the second trigger. Imogen cannot review Dev's build by reading it; she would have to click into every helper column and reconstruct the logic from cell references. When someone else has to sign off on how a number was made, the steps need to be written somewhere a person reads top to bottom. That is what SQL and scripts are, before they are anything else: the method written down.

Self-studyA lookup chain is a join you cannot read3 min▶

Session 2 showed that every lookup is a left join you made without writing the word: each order row keeps its row and picks up one customer, and when the key repeats, the first match wins. One lookup is easy to hold in your head. Three tables means two lookups, plus the cleaning columns each key needs, plus the decision about which side's duplicates matter. By then the join exists only as a pattern of cell references spread across columns, and the rule "first match wins" is applied silently at every one.

Joining more than two tables is where the third question in the tree fires. The written form of the same join names both tables, names the key, and makes "what happens when nothing matches" a choice someone typed rather than a default nobody saw. Ferncastle's Monday numbers need only orders and customers, one join, so on this question alone Dev could stay. He is leaving for the first two reasons.

Real world

A manual process, copied by hand. JPMorgan's 2013 management task force on the "London Whale" losses is reported to describe a value-at-risk spreadsheet that "divided by their sum instead of their average", after a process that involved copying and pasting data by hand. The report itself was not read here, so treat the detail as reported. The pattern is the one in the tree: a calculation repeated on a schedule, by hand, with no written method a reviewer could read, is exactly the work that should have left the grid first.

Part 3 · covers what leaving looks like

The same question, written down

5 min live

Leaving a spreadsheet does not mean throwing away what you learned in it. The grain sentence, the clean keys, the day-first dates, the one-row-per-order rule and the reconciliation all travel. What changes is where the method lives: in cells you click, or in lines you read. No SQL is taught tonight; the point is to see the shape of the move.

LiveHand over, do not reteach3 min▶

Writing joins down is a discipline of its own, and it has its own home. Learn SQL with Phoebe ↗ owns querying: joins with the key named, what happens to unmatched rows chosen on purpose, grouping and summing in one readable statement. Learn Python Data Analysis with Phoebe ↗ owns the script route with pandas, for work that also needs files fetched, cleaned and saved on a schedule. Take this session's four questions to either one; they decide which you need, not how to write it.

There is a halfway house inside Google Sheets. Google's help page describes the QUERY function as one that "Runs a Google Visualization API Query Language query across data". It exists only in Sheets, and it is mentioned here so you recognise it, not taught: if you find yourself writing long QUERY strings that someone else must review, the third question in the tree has already fired.

Self-studyThe same three questions in a shell3 min▶

Ferncastle's rows are not locked inside a spreadsheet. The same orders.csv, customers.csv and items.csv ship with Learn the Command Line with Phoebe ↗, which answers Imogen's three questions (revenue by state, orders per customer, late share) with pipes instead of formulas. The files hold exactly the rows behind every number on these pages, so the truth does not change with the tool: 1,508 orders, R$ 80,469.61 in SP, 7.23% late.

Working the same question in two tools is the fastest way to learn what each one hides. A shell will not pad an ID with zeros for you or guess a date format, so the type traps show up early. A spreadsheet shows every row at once, so the grain is easier to see. Neither is the right tool for everything; the questions in Part 2 tell you which one fits the job in front of you.

Real world

The numbers that make a move safe. Ferncastle's audited build prints R$ 207,300.84 of total revenue and a control gap of R$ 0.00 on 1,508 orders. Those two figures, computed from the shipped rows, are the acceptance test for any replacement: a SQL query or a script that prints anything else has not reproduced the build yet, however tidy it looks. Moving tools without a target number to hit is how a working answer turns back into a plausible one.

Build-along

Write the leave memo for Ferncastle's Monday numbers

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open your cleaned copy of Ferncastle's orders.csv from sessions 1 to 4 (the CSVs are downloadable from session 1) in Excel or Google Sheets, with the running count in column J. Dev writes a one-page memo answering the four questions, makes a call, and attaches the checklist that the next tool must pass. The counts are computed from the shipped rows; the memo's judgement calls are Dev's, and yours may differ.

Count what the build holds. Start the memo with the size facts, measured rather than remembered. Rows from session 1's COUNTA, distinct orders from the running count in column J.

★ Step 1 · size, both appsRows: =COUNTA(A2:A1566) -> 1565 Distinct orders: =COUNTIFS(J2:J1566, 1) -> 1508 Customers file: 1,370 rows (one per customer)

Measure the headroom against each ceiling. Both formulas run in both apps; what they mean depends on the app. The first compares the column with Excel's last row, the second counts the cells the file occupies for the Sheets limit. orders.csv spans A to I.

★ Step 2 · headroomExcel rows: =COUNTA(A:A)/1048576 -> 0.0015 (about 0.15%) Sheets cells: =ROWS(A1:I1566)*COLUMNS(A1:I1566) -> 14094 =ROWS(A1:I1566)*COLUMNS(A1:I1566)/20000000 -> 0.0007 (about 0.07%) Memo line: size is not a reason to leave

Answer question one: does it repeat? List every manual step of the Monday run in order. If a step would be skipped by someone in a hurry without the sheet noticing, mark it.

★ Step 3 · what repeatsEvery Monday: 1 import orders.csv and customers.csv 2 clean customer_id =VALUE(TRIM(C2)) 3 rebuild delivered_date =DATE(RIGHT(E2,4), MID(E2,4,2), LEFT(E2,2)) 4 look up state, exact (XLOOKUP or INDEX/MATCH with 0) 5 fill the running count =COUNTIFS(A$2:A2, A2) 6 refresh the pivot, filter J = 1 7 check the control gap (must be R$ 0.00) Answer: yes, 7 manual steps, every week

Answer questions two and three: review and joins. Name the reviewer and what they need to see. Then count the tables the three numbers actually use; items.csv is not needed for any of them.

★ Step 4 · review and joinsReviewer: Imogen, every week, before the numbers go out She needs: the method in reading order, not cell references Tables: orders + customers (items not used) Joins: 1 (orders.customer_id -> customers.customer_id) Answer: review yes; joins no (two tables, not more)

Make the call and say why. Walk the tree from Part 2 with your four answers. Write the verdict in one sentence and the reason in another. This is Dev's call for a constructed case; a team with no SQL access might pick the script route instead, and that is a fair answer too.

★ Step 5 · the verdictRepeat? yes Review? yes Joins > 2? no Size? no Verdict: move the Monday build to SQL; the sheet becomes the reading copy Imogen opens, not the place it is built Why: it repeats weekly and someone else signs it off, so the method has to be written where she can read it

Attach the audit checklist. Copy the rung-5 numbers into the memo as the acceptance test. The new build is not done until it prints every one of them, with the same checks the spreadsheet needed. Session 6 shows that the audited rung cleans the customers table side too, which is why the first line says both sides.

★ Step 6 · carry the audit across[ ] key cleaned on BOTH sides (orders and customers) [ ] delivered_date parsed day-first, never by the computer's setting [ ] one row per order before any sum -> 1508 orders [ ] unmatched rows counted, not hidden -> 0 [ ] customers 1370, orders per customer -> 1.101 [ ] revenue SP 80469.61, total 207300.84 [ ] late share 7.23% (109 of 1508) [ ] control gap -> 0.00, or it does not ship

Keep the spreadsheet as the reference build. Do not delete it the day the query works. Run both for a few Mondays and compare every number on the checklist. When they agree week after week, the sheet can retire; when they disagree, you have a reconciliation to do, and now you know how.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Run the four questions on one build of your own. A report you produce on a schedule. Write one line per question (repeat, review, joins, size) and the verdict the tree gives. Bring any case where your gut and the tree disagree.
  2. Measure your biggest file's headroom. Rows against 1,048,576 if it lives in Excel, cells against 20 million if it lives in Sheets. Write the percentage in your notes next to the file's name.
  3. Count the hidden joins. In a workbook you inherited, list every lookup column and the table it reaches into. If the list names more than two tables, sketch the written join as four plain-language lines, like the right panel in Part 3.
  4. Write an acceptance test. For one number you hand over regularly, write the two or three control totals any replacement build would have to match, with today's values.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does Google Drive Help give as the size limit for a spreadsheet created in or converted to Google Sheets?

The 10 million figure is out of date, and 1,048,576 by 16,384 is Excel's per-worksheet limit from Microsoft's specifications page. Sheets counts cells across the spreadsheet, so a wide file reaches the ceiling in fewer rows than a narrow one.

2 · Ferncastle's orders.csv has 1,565 rows and the Monday numbers use two tables. Which is the strongest reason to move the build out of the spreadsheet?

1,565 rows is about 0.15% of Excel's rows, and the three numbers need only orders and customers, one join. What fires is repetition (seven manual steps every Monday) and review (a second person must read the method). The audited spreadsheet is correct; the problem is keeping it correct and readable every week.

3 · What did Public Health England's October 2020 statement give as the cause of 15,841 cases not being included?

Press reports named Excel and a row limit; the statement itself names only a maximum file size. Quote sources for what they say. The usable lesson holds either way: a size ceiling dropped records and the published figure looked complete until it was reconciled.
